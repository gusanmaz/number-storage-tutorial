How do computers store data?

Every key you press, every color on screen and every number in your calculator is, deep inside the computer, nothing but 0s and 1s. In this course you will learn how that is possible — step by step, by poking at things.

Why are there different number systems?

We humans use the decimal system — most likely because we have ten fingers. The billions of tiny switches (transistors) inside a computer, however, can only tell two states apart: current or no current. Two digits are enough to write two states: 0 and 1. That is why computers use the binary system.

But long bit strings like 1111111111111111 are tiring and error-prone for people to read. So programmers group bits in fours and write them compactly in hexadecimal: the same value in hex is simply FFFF.

Think first

If you had only 3 lamps, each of which could be on or off, how many different “messages” could you show?

Show answer

Each lamp has 2 options: 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 messages (000, 001, 010, 011, 100, 101, 110, 111). This idea — n bits give 2ⁿ different values — will follow you through the whole course.

Notation

The base of a number can be ambiguous: is 10 ten, two or sixteen? In this course we show the base as a small subscript: 10102 = 128 = 1010 = A16. They are all the same number. Colors help too: pink = binary, green = octal, blue = decimal, orange = hex.

How to get the most out of this course

  • Play. Every chapter has boxes labelled “Interactive”. Change values, step through animations, watch what happens.
  • Predict, then check. In the “Think first” boxes, make your own guess before revealing the answer. That is where most learning happens.
  • Use pen and paper. Doing a few conversions by hand is the fastest and most durable way to learn.
  • Do the exercises. Questions are generated randomly, so you can practise as much as you like. Stuck? Use the “Hint” button.
