Chapter 1

One switch, two worlds

Inside a computer there are no letters, no colors, not even numbers really. There are only billions of tiny switches turning on and off. Everything starts here.

Reading: ~20 minPrerequisites: curiosity

While you read this sentence, switches inside your device are flipping on and off billions of times a second. The shapes of the letters on the screen, the background color, how far down you have scrolled… all of it is held in the current state of those switches. This essay makes one single claim, and we will spend the rest of it unpacking it:

Everything in a computer is patterns of switches plus agreements about how to read those patterns.

Let’s start with the smallest piece: a single switch. Tap the lamp below.

This single switch is called a bit, short for binary digit. A bit is the smallest possible piece of information. There cannot be anything smaller, because something with only one state says nothing: a lamp that never changes cannot tell you anything.

§ 1.1What can one bit say?

Anything with two options: yes or no, true or false, heads or tails, door open or closed. In programming this is called a boolean and written true/false. But a single bit can answer exactly one such question.

HistoryIn 1775 American revolutionaries signaled which route British troops were taking out of Boston with lanterns hung in a church steeple: “One if by land, two if by sea.” That is literally an encoding: an agreement made in advance.

What if we want to say more? Which day of the week it is, say, or a letter, or a color? The answer is simple: we use more switches.

§ 1.2More switches

Suppose we have switches. With them we can make exactly 8 different patterns. Drag the number left and right (or press the arrows) to add and remove switches and watch how the patterns multiply.

This doubling is perhaps the most used fact in computer science. 1 switch, 2 patterns; 2 switches, 4; 3 switches, 8… Each time we multiply by two:

n switches → 2 × 2 × … × 2 = 2n patterns

Doubling looks slow at first but speeds up incredibly. 3 switches: 8 patterns.

§ 1.3One bit = one yes/no question

The nicest way to understand a bit is a game. Think of a number between 0 and 127. I will ask you only yes/no questions and find it in at most seven.

Why are seven questions enough? Because every “yes” or “no” cuts the remaining possibilities exactly in half. You start with 128 numbers; after one question 64 remain, then 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1. Seven halvings get you down to one number, and 27 = 128.

This game gives us the true meaning of a bit: a bit is the answer to a well-asked yes/no question. Whenever you wonder how many bits it takes to encode something, ask yourself: “How many yes/no questions would I need to pin it down?”

HistoryMeasuring information this way was proposed by Claude Shannon in 1948; his paper “A Mathematical Theory of Communication” is the foundation of modern communication. The paper also popularized the word “bit”, which Shannon credited to his colleague John Tukey.

5 questions distinguish 25 = 32 possibilities: not enough. 6 questions distinguish 64: enough. So 6 bits. The general rule: to tell N things apart you need the smallest n with 2n ≥ N.

§ 1.4Why two? Why not ten?

We count on ten fingers. Why not build computers that work with ten voltage levels and think directly in decimal? The answer is physics: noise. Electrical signals are never perfect; temperature, interference between wires, even cosmic rays nudge the voltage a little. Below, watch two systems send a message under the same noise.

With two levels the receiver has only one question: “Is the voltage high or low?” A wide safety margin is left in between. That robustness is what lets billions of transistors work flawlessly billions of times every second. On top of that, a two-state switch is far simpler, smaller and cheaper to make than a ten-state one.

§ 1.5Can switches think?

Choosing two states brings one more gift. In 1854 the mathematician George Boole turned logic into an algebra over just two values, “true” and “false”. In 1937 Claude Shannon, still a master’s student, showed that circuits made of switches compute exactly this algebra. Tap the two switches below:

So switches do not just store information, they can process it too: a circuit that adds two numbers is really just AND, OR and XOR gates wired the right way. This essay focuses on storing rather than processing, but never forget that both are done by the same switches.

§ 1.6Where do bits live?

“Switch” is a metaphor. In reality a bit is any physical thing whose two states can be told apart reliably:

Where“0”“1”

§ 1.7A pattern means nothing by itself

Now we reach the most important idea. Below are eight lamps. What does the pattern they make mean? The surprising answer: nothing, on its own. The meaning is set by the agreement used by whoever reads the pattern. Play with the lamps and switch between the different “lenses”.

That is why this is more than a “number systems” lesson. Each chapter takes up one agreement: reading patterns as numbers, negative numbers, fractions, letters, colors and sounds. In the end we will see how they all sit side by side on one long strip of bytes.

§ 1.8Try it yourself

  • 8

  • 10
Two: 0 and 1. Something with a single value carries no information.

If you add one switch, what happens to the number of patterns?

  • It grows by 1
  • It doubles
  • It is squared
  • It does not change
All the old patterns can be made once with the new switch off and once with it on: twice as many.

What is the main reason computers use binary?

  • Binary arithmetic is easy for people
  • A tradition that saves energy
  • They were built before decimal was invented
  • Telling two states apart is far more reliable under noise
Two levels leave a wide safety margin; small noise does not spoil the reading. Two-state switches are also simple and small.

What does the pattern 01000001 represent?

  • It depends on the agreement used to read it
  • Always the number 65
  • Always the letter “A”
  • Nothing at all
As a number 65, as an ASCII letter “A”, as a gray level a dark gray… The reader decides.
