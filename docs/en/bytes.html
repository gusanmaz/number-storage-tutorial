Chapter 3

Bytes and the measure of information

Working with single bits is like reading a book letter by letter. Computers move bits around in packs of eight. This chapter is about that pack, its limits, and how we measure the size of data.

Reading: ~20 minBefore: Chapter 2

§ 3.1Why eight?

A group of bits is called a byte, and today almost everywhere a byte is 8 bits. But that is not a law of nature. Computers of the 1950s used 6-bit characters and 36-bit “words”. In 1956 IBM engineer Werner Buchholz named groups of bits bytes, spelling it with a y instead of “bite” so it would not be confused with “bit”. In 1964 the IBM System/360 family used 8-bit bytes, and that became the standard.

Why is eight a good choice? It does three jobs at once: it is enough to store a letter (with upper and lower case, digits and punctuation), it is a power of two (handy for hardware), and it splits into exactly two hex digits. Play with the byte below.

A byte can make 28 = 256 different patterns. Those patterns can be the numbers 0 to 255, but remember the lens from Chapter 1: the same 256 patterns can just as well be a letter, a shade of gray, the red part of a color, or (as we will see in Chapter 5) a number from −128 to 127.

TermsNibble: 4 bits (half a byte). Word: the size a processor handles in one go; mostly 64 bits today. Byte: the smallest unit with its own address in memory; you cannot address a single bit directly.

§ 3.2How many bits do you need?

In Chapter 1 we defined a bit as “one yes/no question”. The same idea answers the most practical question: how many bits do you need to tell N different things apart? Every bit halves the possibilities; you need as many bits as the number of halvings it takes to get down to a single possibility.

Since the number of bits must be a whole number, some patterns usually stay unused. Numbering 1000 students takes 10 bits, but 10 bits give 1024 patterns: 24 of them are never used. The waste is unavoidable; there is no such thing as 9.97 bits.

§ 3.3Fixed width: what happens when the box is full?

On paper a number can have as many digits as you like; you just add digits to the left. In a computer every number has a fixed-size “box”: 8, 16, 32 or 64 bits. To see what happens when the box fills up, push the 8-bit counter below past 255.

§ 3.4Is a kilo 1000 or 1024?

Because computers work with powers of two, 210 = 1024 was long called “kilo”; it was so close to 1000. But in the SI system kilo always means 1000 (kilometer, kilogram). Watch the small difference grow as the units get bigger:

Disk makers use decimal units; Windows computes in binary units but labels the result “GB”. The result: a disk that says GB on the box shows up in Windows as GB. Not a single byte is missing; two different “kilos” are in use. (macOS has used decimal units since 2009 and shows the capacity printed on the box.)

§ 3.5Bits or bytes?

One more trap: internet speeds are measured in bits (lowercase b: Mbit/s, Mbps), file sizes in bytes (uppercase B: MB). That is a factor of 8. On a Mbit/s connection a GB movie downloads in at best ( MB per second).

What happens if you add 1 to 255 in an 8-bit counter?

  • It becomes 256
  • It stays at 255
  • The program crashes
  • It becomes 0
There is no room for a ninth bit; the carry is lost and the counter wraps around.
