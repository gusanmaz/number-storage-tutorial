Sound

Sound is a continuous vibration in the air; a computer can only store numbers. The bridge between them: measure the wave thousands of times per second and record each measurement as an integer.

Sound is a wave

When a guitar string vibrates it rapidly pushes air pressure up and down. A microphone turns this pressure change into a continuously varying voltage. That signal is analog: at every instant it can take any one of infinitely many values. Since a computer cannot store numbers with infinite precision, we digitize the signal in two steps:

1. Sampling

Measure the wave at equal intervals. The number of measurements per second is the sample rate (Hz).

2. Quantization

Round each measurement to the nearest integer level. The number of levels is set by the bit depth: n bits → 2ⁿ levels.

Things to try
  • Drop the bit depth to 1: the wave becomes a square wave and sounds buzzy — a lot like old game consoles.
  • Sample the 3000 Hz tone at 4000 Hz: the dots actually trace a much slower wave! This is called aliasing.

The Nyquist rule: how often should we measure?

To capture a wave correctly you need at least two measurements per cycle. So the sample rate must be at least twice the highest frequency you want to record. This is the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem.

sample rate > 2 × highest frequency Human hearing covers roughly 20 Hz – 20,000 Hz → 2 × 20,000 = 40,000. CDs use 44,100 Hz to leave some margin.

Phone calls are sampled at 8,000 Hz, so only sounds up to about 4,000 Hz get through. That’s why voices sound “muffled” on the phone: the highs are cut off.

Each sample is usually stored as a signed integer: −32,768 … 32,767 at 16 bits. The downward parts of the wave are negative numbers (remember the Negative Numbers chapter!).

How many bytes is an audio file?

bytes = sample rate × (bit depth ÷ 8) × channels × seconds

MP3 and compression

A 3-minute CD track is about 30 MB. Formats like MP3, AAC and Opus discard details the ear cannot hear or that are masked by other sounds (lossy compression), shrinking it to about 3 MB. FLAC discards nothing (lossless) and roughly halves it.

Summary

  • Sound is digitized in two steps: sampling in time and quantization in amplitude.
  • The sample rate must exceed twice the highest frequency (Nyquist). CD: 44,100 Hz.
  • n-bit depth → 2ⁿ levels. CD: 16 bits; samples are signed integers.
  • Raw size = rate × bits/8 × channels × duration. CD audio is 176,400 bytes per second (1,411 kbit/s).

To record sounds up to 15,000 Hz, what must the sample rate be at least?

  • 15,000 Hz
  • More than 30,000 Hz
  • 7,500 Hz
  • It must be exactly 44,100 Hz
Nyquist: more than twice the highest frequency → above 30,000 Hz.

How many different levels can a 4-bit audio sample take?

  • 4
  • 8
  • 16
  • 256
2⁴ = 16 levels.

How many bytes does 1 second of 8,000 Hz, 8-bit, mono audio take?

  • 8,000
  • 64,000
  • 16,000
  • 1,000
8,000 × 1 byte × 1 channel × 1 s = 8,000 bytes.

What does lowering the bit depth do to sound?

  • Makes it higher-pitched
  • Slows it down
  • Cuts off high frequencies
  • Rounding error grows; the sound becomes noisy/buzzy
Fewer levels = each sample is rounded more coarsely = quantization noise.
